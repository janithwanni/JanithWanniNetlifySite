Seeing what the explainers see

17 Oct 2026 · 3 min read

Ask four explainable AI (XAI) methods why a model made a prediction and you can get four different answers. That isn’t a bug in any one of them. Each method looks at a different facet of the model, but they usually come back to us as a bar chart of numbers, which hides the fact that each is answering a different question.

This post summarises the first project of my PhD (chapter 2 of my thesis), done with Dianne Cook, Kate Saunders, Patricia Menendez and Thiyanga Talagala. The idea is simple: put the explanations back where the data lives. To scroll through the example with the real figures, read the interactive version, Where the explainers point.

Four explainers, three shared parts

Local explainers try to answer “why did the model predict this for this observation?” Most of them have the same three parts:

  1. A perturbation mechanism: some way of generating nearby, “what-if” points.
  2. An optimisation objective: what the method is trying to find among those points.
  3. A structured output: the form the answer comes back in.

The methods differ in what they choose for each part, and that’s where the disagreement comes from.

Explainer What it looks for Its shape in the data space
SHAP Each feature’s share of the prediction Arrows parallel to the feature axes
LIME A simple model that mimics the black box nearby A local linear decision boundary
Counterfactuals The closest point that changes the prediction Another point, with a path to it
Anchors A rule that “locks in” the prediction A box around the observation

Once each output is drawn as a geometric object (an arrow, a line, a point or a box), you can lay all four over the same scatterplot and over the model’s own decision boundary.

A small, honest example

To make the comparison easy to check, we used a simulated two-variable classification problem with a known, rule-based decision boundary. The model was a random forest of 10 trees, and we explained four observations. Because the true boundary is known, you can see each explanation next to the truth.

Two things stand out:

  • The methods often disagree on which feature matters most, even for the same point and the same model.
  • Each explanation makes sense in its own terms. An Anchors box that hugs a boundary corner and SHAP arrows that point mostly along one axis aren’t contradicting each other. They answer different questions.

To compare the numbers directly, we also put the methods’ feature-importance outputs on a common scale. The pictures do most of the explaining, though.

Beyond two dimensions

Real models rarely have two features. For higher dimensions, the same geometric objects can be shown in tours: animated sequences of linear projections that let you look at high-dimensional data from many angles. An Anchors box becomes a hyper-rectangle and a LIME boundary becomes a hyperplane, and a tour shows how they sit relative to the data and the model. This is part of why I rebuilt Anchors and LIME in R (see the next post): the geometry has to be accessible for it to be drawn.

Why this matters

If you use XAI to justify a lending decision, a medical triage rule or a climate-risk score, “the explainer said so” isn’t enough. Seeing the explanation against the data tells you what kind of claim each method is making, and when two methods disagree, it tells you why. My message isn’t to pick the best explainer. Use several, and look at them together.
